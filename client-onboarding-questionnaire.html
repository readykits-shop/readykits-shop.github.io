Client Onboarding Questionnaire for Freelancers: 15 Questions to Ask Before You Start

Most freelance projects that go wrong don't go wrong at the end. They go wrong in the first week, when nobody asked who signs off on the work, what "done" actually means, or who pays the invoices. A short onboarding questionnaire fixes that. You send it once a client says yes, before the contract and the deposit, and you get everything you need in writing.

Here are 15 questions worth asking, why each one matters, and how to use the answers.

Why use a questionnaire instead of a call?

A call is great for building rapport, but a written questionnaire has advantages:

  • It's on record. When scope questions come up later, you can point to what the client wrote.
  • It makes the client think. Many clients haven't decided what they want until they have to write it down.
  • It saves time. You arrive at the kickoff call already knowing the basics, and spend the call on the parts that need discussion.
  • It looks professional. A clear process tells the client they're in good hands.

The best approach is often both: send the questionnaire first, then hold a short call to go through anything unclear.

About the client

1. What's your company or brand name, and what do you do? The basics, but also context. One or two sentences in their own words often tells you how they see themselves, which matters for anything creative.

2. Who is the main contact, and how do you prefer to communicate? Email, Slack, phone? Agree on one channel up front, so feedback doesn't end up spread across four apps.

3. Who approves the final work? This is one of the most important questions on the list. If the person you work with day to day isn't the one who signs off, you need to know now. Otherwise the "final" version gets rejected by a manager you've never heard from.

4. Who else will give feedback? More reviewers means more rounds of changes. Knowing this helps you plan revisions and price the project properly.

The project

5. What problem should this project solve? Not "we need a new website", but why: more leads, a rebrand, an outdated site that embarrasses the sales team. The problem shapes every decision you make.

6. What does success look like in 90 days? This turns vague goals into something measurable, like more enquiries, a launched product or a finished campaign. It also gives you a clear story for your portfolio afterward.

7. Who is the audience or end user? You can't design, write or build well for "everyone". Ask who the work is really for.

8. What examples do you like and dislike, and why? Links are worth more than adjectives. "Modern and clean" means something different to everyone. Ask for the why too, because two clients can like the same example for opposite reasons.

9. What's in scope, and what's out of scope? List the deliverables, and just as importantly, what isn't included. This answer feeds straight into your proposal and contract. The guide to writing a freelance proposal shows how to turn it into clear packages.

Timeline and budget

10. Is there a hard deadline, and why? A launch event or a trade show is a real deadline. "As soon as possible" usually isn't. Knowing the reason tells you how fixed the date really is, and whether a rush fee applies.

11. What's your budget range? Many freelancers skip this because it feels awkward. Don't. A range saves both sides from a proposal that was never going to work. If they won't share one, offer two or three options at different price points. The freelance rate calculator guide helps you work out your minimum.

Logistics

12. What assets and access will I need from you? Logins, brand files, logos, fonts, existing copy, analytics access. Ask early, because waiting for assets is one of the most common causes of delay. Agree on secure ways to share passwords rather than plain email.

13. Who handles billing, and what details go on the invoice? The billing contact's name and email, the company's legal name and address for the invoice, any purchase order number, and whether they need a supplier form or tax form first. This one question prevents a lot of late payments. The late-payment playbook explains why.

14. How and when will you give feedback? Agree on response times (for example, feedback within 3 business days) and the format: one consolidated list, comments in the file, or a call. Your contract can then say that deadlines move if feedback is late.

15. Is there anything else I should know? Always end with an open question. This is where you hear about the previous freelancer who disappeared, the internal politics, or the board meeting next month. All of it is useful.

How to use the answers

Once the questionnaire comes back:

  1. Read it for red flags. No budget, no decision-maker, "we need it next week", or answers that contradict each other. Raise them now, not mid-project.
  2. Write the proposal from it. Scope, deliverables, timeline and price should all trace back to their answers.
  3. Write the contract from the proposal. The guide to freelance contract clauses covers what to include, from scope and deposits to revisions and ownership.
  4. Set up billing. Add the client's billing contact and invoice details to your records, so the first invoice goes to the right person. The invoice template guide covers what else belongs on it.
  5. Hold a short kickoff call to confirm the plan and go through anything unclear.

Tips for sending it

  • Keep it short. Fifteen questions is a ceiling, not a target. Cut anything you don't need for a particular project.
  • Send it as an editable document or form, so the client can fill it in quickly.
  • Set a deadline, such as "please return this before our kickoff call on Thursday".
  • Make it part of every project, even with returning clients. A quick refresh catches changes in goals, budget or contacts.
