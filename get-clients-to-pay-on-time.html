How to Get Clients to Pay on Time: A Freelancer's Late-Payment Playbook

Late payments are one of the most common problems freelancers run into, and one of the most stressful. The good news is that most late invoices aren't caused by bad clients. They come from unclear terms, invoices sent at the wrong time, and follow-ups that never happen. Fix those three things and most of the problem goes away.

This playbook covers what to set up before the work starts, how to time and word your invoices, a simple reminder schedule, and what to do when a client still doesn't pay.

General information, not legal or financial advice. Rules on late fees and interest vary by country and US state, so check what applies to you.

1. Get paid before you start: deposits

The easiest late payment to deal with is the one that can't happen. For new clients, ask for a deposit before any work begins. 30–50% is common, and 50% is reasonable for short projects or first-time clients.

A deposit does three things:

  • It confirms the client is serious and has the budget.
  • It covers your time if the project stalls or is cancelled.
  • It sets the expectation, from day one, that invoices from you get paid.

For longer projects, split the rest into milestones (for example 40% deposit, 30% at the first draft, 30% on delivery) so you never have a large amount of unpaid work outstanding. For ongoing retainers, bill at the start of each month, not the end.

The rule that saves the most money: don't start until the contract is signed and the deposit has landed. Not "the client says it's on its way". Landed.

2. Make the payment terms impossible to miss

Vague terms like "payment on receipt" or "pay when you can" invite delays. Put specific terms in your contract and on every invoice:

  • Due date as a calendar date, not just "Net 14". "Due: March 14" is much harder to forget.
  • Payment window. Net 7 or Net 14 is common for freelancers. Large companies may insist on Net 30 or longer, so agree on it up front rather than finding out later.
  • How to pay. A payment link, bank details, or both. Every extra step a client has to take is a reason to put it off.
  • Late fee, if you use one (see section 5).
  • Your right to pause work while invoices are overdue.

The guide to freelance contract clauses has example wording for deposits, late fees and pausing work, and the freelance invoice template guide covers everything that should be on the invoice itself.

3. Invoice at the right time

Timing matters more than most freelancers think.

  • Invoice the same day you deliver, while the client is happy with the work. An invoice that arrives three weeks later feels like an afterthought, and gets treated like one.
  • Send it to the right person. Ask during onboarding who handles billing. Many invoices sit unpaid simply because they went to a project contact who doesn't pay bills. (Our client onboarding questionnaire guide includes this question.)
  • Ask about their process. Some companies need a purchase order number, a supplier form or a tax form before they can pay you. Find out before your first invoice, not after it bounces.
  • Use clear invoice numbers and a short subject line, like "Invoice INV-0014 – Website redesign – due March 14", so it's easy to find and forward.

4. A reminder schedule you actually follow

Most late payments are fixed by a polite reminder. The trick is to send them on a fixed schedule, so you aren't deciding each time whether it's "too soon" to ask. A simple schedule:

When What you send
3 days before due (optional) A friendly heads-up that the invoice is due on Friday
1 day overdue A polite reminder, with the invoice attached again and the payment link
7 days overdue A firmer reminder: the late fee now applies (if your contract has one), and work pauses until it's paid
14 days overdue A final notice with the total due, the new deadline, and the next step if it isn't paid

Track every invoice's due date and days overdue in one place, and check it once a week. The income and expense tracking guide includes a simple spreadsheet formula that counts overdue days automatically.

5. Late fees: useful, but set them up properly

A late fee gives your reminders weight. A common form is a monthly percentage on the overdue balance, for example 1.5% per month. A few rules:

  • It has to be in the contract the client agreed to. Adding a late fee to an invoice out of nowhere usually isn't enforceable and damages the relationship.
  • Put the same wording on every invoice, so it's never a surprise.
  • Check local limits. Some US states and countries cap interest or late charges, and some places have their own statutory late-payment rules for business invoices.
  • Use it as leverage, not revenue. Many freelancers waive the fee once when a good client pays promptly after a reminder. That's fine. The point is that the fee exists.

6. Reminder email wording that stays polite

Keep reminders short, factual and friendly. Assume it's an oversight, because it usually is.

1 day overdue:

Subject: Invoice INV-0014 – due yesterday

Hi Sam, a quick reminder that invoice INV-0014 for $1,200 was due yesterday (March 14). I've attached it again, and you can pay here: [payment link]. Let me know if you need anything from me to process it. Thanks!

7 days overdue:

Subject: Invoice INV-0014 – 7 days overdue

Hi Sam, invoice INV-0014 for $1,200 is now 7 days overdue. As per our agreement, a late fee of 1.5% per month now applies, and I'll pause work on the project until the balance is paid. Could you confirm when payment will be sent? Payment link: [payment link].

14 days overdue:

Subject: Final notice – invoice INV-0014

Hi Sam, invoice INV-0014 is now 14 days overdue, and work is paused. The total due is $1,200 plus the late fee under our agreement. Please arrange payment by March 31. If there's a problem with the invoice or the budget, reply and let's sort it out. Otherwise I'll need to take the next step to recover the amount.

If you get no reply at all, try a different channel (a phone call or a message to the billing contact) before anything formal. Silence is often an inbox problem, not a refusal.

7. When to pause work

Pausing work is your strongest tool, and it's fair, as long as your contract says you can do it. A clear rule makes it less personal:

  • Pause new work at 7 days overdue. Finish anything you've promised for that day, then stop until the balance is paid.
  • Don't hand over final files or transfer ownership until the final invoice is paid. Many contracts tie ownership of the deliverables to full payment, for exactly this reason.
  • Tell the client in writing, calmly: what's paused, why, and what restarts it.
  • For repeat late payers, switch to paying upfront or larger deposits for future work.

If a client still doesn't pay after the final notice, your options include a formal demand letter, a collections service, or small claims court, depending on the amount and where you both are. At that stage, it's worth getting local advice.

The short version

  1. Take a deposit and don't start before it lands.
  2. Put a calendar due date, payment link, late fee and pause clause in the contract and on every invoice.
  3. Invoice on delivery, to the person who pays bills.
  4. Send reminders at 1, 7 and 14 days overdue, every time.
  5. Pause work at 7 days overdue, and hold final files until the last invoice is paid.
