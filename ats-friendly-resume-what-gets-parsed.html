ATS-Friendly Resume: What Actually Gets Parsed (and What Breaks)

When you apply online, your resume usually lands in an applicant tracking system (ATS) first. The ATS turns your file into plain text, tries to split that text into fields like name, job titles, dates and skills, and stores it so recruiters can search and filter. A person still reads the resumes, but they often read the version the software built, or find you through a keyword search.

So the real question isn't "will a robot reject me?". It's simpler: when the software pulls the text out of my file, does it come out complete and in the right order? This guide covers what reliably parses, what tends to break, and a quick test you can run on your own resume.

General information. Every ATS works a little differently, and none of this guarantees an interview. It just removes avoidable problems.

First, a myth to drop

You may have seen claims that a huge share of resumes are "auto-rejected by ATS". There isn't solid public evidence for a single number like that, and most systems don't throw resumes away on their own. What actually happens is less dramatic but still costly: a scrambled or half-empty profile is harder to find in a search and looks worse when a recruiter opens it. That's the problem worth fixing.

What parses cleanly

These are the things nearly every parser handles well:

  • One column, top to bottom. Text in a single flow is read in the same order you see it.
  • Plain paragraphs and real bullet points. Word's built-in bullet formatting comes out as clean lines of text.
  • Standard section headings. "Professional Summary", "Skills", "Work Experience", "Education" and "Certifications" are what parsers are built to recognize.
  • Contact details in the body of the page. Name on the first line, then city and state, phone, email and LinkedIn on the next lines.
  • A consistent job format. Job title, company, location and dates in the same order for every role, then the bullets.
  • Common fonts. Calibri, Arial, Helvetica or Garamond at 10 to 12 pt.
  • A consistent date format. For example "Mar 2021 – Present" everywhere, rather than a mix of styles.

What breaks (or can break) parsing

Not every system fails on every item below, but each one is a common cause of scrambled or missing text.

Tables and multi-column layouts

Two-column designs often put skills or contact details in a sidebar. Some parsers read across the page row by row, so a line of your sidebar gets glued to a line of your job history. Others read one column fully and then the other, which can separate job titles from their dates. If your resume is built from a table, the reading order depends on how the table was made, not how it looks.

Text boxes, shapes and floating objects

Text inside a text box or shape is stored separately from the main text of the document. Some parsers skip it entirely, others drop it at the very end. If your name or contact details sit in a text box, they may be missing from the profile.

Headers and footers

Putting your name and contact details in the page header looks neat, but some systems ignore headers and footers. Keep anything important in the body of the page.

Icons, images, logos and skill bars

A phone icon next to your number is fine for a human and meaningless to software: it's an image, not text. The same goes for logos, charts and "skill bars" that show 4 out of 5 dots. If the information matters, write it as words, for example "Advanced Excel (pivot tables, XLOOKUP)".

Creative headings

"My Journey" or "What I Bring" are harder to match to a standard section than "Work Experience" or "Skills". Creativity belongs in your bullets, not your headings.

Scanned or image-only PDFs

If your PDF was made by scanning or exporting as an image, there's no text in it to read. A PDF saved directly from Word or Google Docs contains real text, which is fine unless the job post asks for a .docx.

Unusual fonts and special characters

Decorative fonts, unusual symbols as bullets, and some ligatures (like "fi" joined into one character) can occasionally come out as odd characters. Standard fonts and Word's own bullet styles avoid this.

A 2-minute test you can run yourself

You don't need special software to spot most problems:

  1. Open your resume and select all the text (Ctrl+A or Cmd+A), then copy it.
  2. Paste it into a plain text editor like Notepad or TextEdit (in plain text mode).
  3. Read it top to bottom and check:
  4. Is your name the first line, followed by your contact details?
  5. Are the section headings there, in order?
  6. Does each job title sit next to its company and dates?
  7. Is anything missing, such as a sidebar, a text box or header text?
  8. Are there strange characters where bullets or symbols used to be?

If the plain text reads correctly from top to bottom, a parser will usually read it correctly too. If it's jumbled, simplify the layout before you send it anywhere.

Keywords: match the job post honestly

Once your file parses cleanly, the content matters most. Recruiters often search their ATS for job titles, skills and tools, so:

  • Use the exact job title from the post under your name, where it honestly describes your work.
  • Mirror the tool and skill names the post uses, such as "Google Analytics 4" rather than only "analytics tools".
  • Spell out acronyms once, for example "Search Engine Optimization (SEO)", so both versions can be matched.
  • Put numbers in your bullets (time saved, money saved, percentages, volume), and only use numbers you can explain in an interview.
  • Don't paste hidden keywords in white text. Recruiters read the extracted text and will see it.

.docx or PDF?

Follow the job post. If it says .docx, send .docx. If it accepts either, a simple single-column .docx is the safest for parsing, and a text-based PDF saved from Word or Google Docs is usually fine too. What matters more than the format is the layout inside it.

Quick checklist

  • One column, no tables, text boxes, icons or images
  • Name and contact details in the body, not the header
  • Standard headings, consistent job format and dates
  • Common font at 10 to 12 pt, real bullet points
  • Keywords and job title matched to the post, honestly
  • Passed the copy-paste test in a plain text editor
