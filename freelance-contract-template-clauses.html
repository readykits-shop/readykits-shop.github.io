Freelance Contract Template: 10 Clauses Every Freelancer Needs

Most freelance disputes aren't about bad clients. They come from things nobody wrote down: what "done" means, when the money is due, what happens if the project gets cancelled halfway through. A short, clear contract settles those questions before they turn into arguments.

This guide walks through the 10 clauses that do the most work in a freelance contract, what each one should say, and some example wording you can adapt.

Not legal advice. This is general information to help you understand common contract terms. Laws differ by country and US state, and some clauses (especially liability limits and late fees) may be limited where you or your client are based. For a high-value or unusual project, have a lawyer in your jurisdiction review your contract.

1. Scope of work

The scope clause is the most important one in the document. List the deliverables, the format and the deadline, and say clearly what's not included.

Example: "Freelancer will deliver a 5-page website design (homepage, about, services, blog template, contact) as Figma files by June 30. Copywriting, development and stock photo licenses are not included."

If you already sent a proposal, the contract can refer to it ("Services as described in the Proposal dated May 2, attached") instead of repeating everything. Our guide on how to write a freelance proposal covers how to lay out the options so the scope is easy to copy across.

2. Payment terms

State the total fee, the deposit, the payment schedule and how you'll be paid.

  • Deposit: 30–50% before work starts is common for new clients.
  • Schedule: a single balance on delivery for small jobs, or milestones (for example 40/30/30) for bigger ones.
  • Due date: "within 14 days of invoice" is clearer than "upon receipt".
  • Method: bank transfer, card or payment link, and who pays transfer fees.

If you're not sure what to charge in the first place, start with the freelance rate calculator guide.

3. Late fees

A late fee gives your reminder emails some weight. A common form is a monthly percentage on the overdue balance, plus the right to pause work.

Example: "Balances unpaid after the due date incur a late fee of 1.5% per month. Freelancer may pause work until overdue invoices are paid."

Two cautions. Some US states and countries cap interest or late charges, so check yours. And only put the late fee on your invoice if the contract says the same thing. The freelance invoice template guide shows how to word it on the invoice itself.

4. Revisions

Unlimited revisions are how a two-week project turns into a three-month one. Say how many rounds are included, what counts as a round, and how extra work is priced.

Example: "The fee includes 2 rounds of revisions. A round is one consolidated set of feedback. Additional rounds or new requests are billed at $X per hour, quoted in writing before work starts."

5. Kill fee (cancellation)

If a client cancels partway through, a kill fee covers the time you've already put in and the other work you turned down to make room.

Example: "If Client cancels the project after work begins, Client pays for all work completed to date, and no less than 25% of the total fee. The deposit is non-refundable."

The right percentage depends on the project. Some freelancers use a sliding scale, such as 25% before the first draft and 50% after it.

6. Intellectual property transfer on payment

Clients usually expect to own what they paid for. That's fair, but tie the transfer to full payment, so you keep leverage if the final invoice is never paid.

Example: "On receipt of full payment, Client owns the final deliverables. Freelancer keeps ownership of pre-existing materials, tools, templates and know-how, and grants Client a license to use any of them included in the deliverables. Freelancer may show the work in a portfolio unless agreed otherwise in writing."

A note for US freelancers: the "work made for hire" rules in US copyright law apply to independent contractors only in specific cases, so many contracts use a written assignment of rights instead. This is one clause worth getting reviewed if a lot of money or valuable IP is involved.

7. Termination

This sets out how either side can end the agreement and what's owed when they do.

Example: "Either party may end this Agreement with 14 days' written notice. Client pays for work completed up to the termination date, plus any kill fee under Section 5. Either party may end it immediately if the other materially breaches the Agreement and doesn't fix it within 7 days of notice."

For ongoing retainers, the notice period also protects your income. It gives you time to replace the client.

8. Confidentiality

Clients often share non-public information: pricing, customer data, product plans. A simple mutual confidentiality clause reassures them and protects you too.

Example: "Both parties keep the other's non-public information confidential during this Agreement and for 2 years after, except where disclosure is required by law."

If the client sends their own NDA, read it. Check that it doesn't quietly stop you from working for others in the same industry, and that it doesn't add one-sided penalties.

9. Limitation of liability

Without a cap, a small project could in theory leave you exposed to a claim far larger than your fee. A liability cap limits your total exposure, usually to the amount the client paid.

Example: "Freelancer's total liability under this Agreement is limited to the fees paid by Client under it. Neither party is liable for indirect or consequential losses."

Many jurisdictions don't allow you to limit liability for things like fraud or gross negligence, so a cap isn't a shield against everything. For risky work (code that handles payments, financial or medical content), think about professional liability insurance as well.

10. Governing law and disputes

Say which country's or state's law applies and how disagreements get handled. This matters most when you and the client are in different places.

Example: "This Agreement is governed by the laws of [State/Country]. The parties will first try to resolve any dispute through good-faith discussion for 30 days before starting formal proceedings."

Picking your own location's law is common, but bigger clients may insist on theirs. Whatever you agree, write it down.

Two clauses worth adding

  • Independent contractor status. You're responsible for your own taxes, tools and schedule, and you're not an employee. In the US, how the work is actually done matters more than the label, but the clause still sets expectations.
  • Client responsibilities. The client supplies content, feedback and approvals by agreed dates, and deadlines move if they're late.

Common contract mistakes

  • Starting work before the contract is signed and the deposit has landed. This is the most common mistake, and the most expensive.
  • Vague deliverables like "social media help" or "a logo and some variations".
  • No written change process. Every "quick extra" should get a written quote.
  • Copying a contract from another country or industry without checking that it fits your work and your local law.
  • A 15-page document for a $500 job. Two clear pages that the client actually reads protect you better than a long one they skim.
