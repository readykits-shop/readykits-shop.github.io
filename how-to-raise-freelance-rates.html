How to Raise Your Freelance Rates Without Losing Good Clients

Most freelancers wait too long to raise their rates. You start low to win your first clients, you get better and faster, your calendar fills up, and you're still charging what you charged on day one. The longer you wait, the bigger the jump has to be, and the scarier it feels.

Raising rates doesn't have to be a confrontation. Done with notice, a clear reason and a simple email, most good clients accept it. This guide covers how to tell it's time, how to work out the new number, how to handle new and existing clients differently, and what to say.

General information, not financial or legal advice. Check your contracts for any notice periods or fixed-price terms before changing rates.

Signs it's time to raise your rates

Two or three of these is usually enough:

  • You're booked out. If you regularly turn work away or have a waiting list, demand is higher than your price.
  • Nobody pushes back. If every proposal is accepted without a question, you're probably cheap for your market.
  • You've got faster or better. Charging by the hour punishes skill: the quicker you get, the less you earn per project.
  • Your costs went up. Software, equipment, insurance and living costs rise every year. A rate that stays flat is a pay cut.
  • It's been a year or more since your last increase.
  • Your numbers don't work. You're busy, but after taxes and expenses you're not hitting the income you need.

That last one matters most, so start there.

Step 1: Work out your real minimum rate

Before choosing a new rate, know your floor: the lowest rate that actually covers your life and your business.

The calculation is simple:

  1. Take-home income you want per year.
  2. Gross it up for tax. If you set aside 28% of profit for tax, divide your income goal by 0.72.
  3. Add business expenses (software, equipment, insurance, accounting, and so on).
  4. Divide by your real billable hours. Not 40 hours a week for 52 weeks. Take off weeks for holidays, sick days and slow periods, and count only the hours you can actually bill. Admin, marketing and emails don't count.

For example, with a $60,000 take-home goal, a 28% tax set-aside, $6,000 of expenses, 6 weeks off and 25 billable hours a week, you need about $89,333 in revenue over 1,150 billable hours. That's a minimum of roughly $78 an hour. If you're charging $50, that gap is your answer.

Step 2: Choose the increase

Your minimum rate is a floor, not a target. Where you land above it depends on your market, your demand and your relationship with each client.

A few practical approaches:

  • A regular increase for existing clients. Many freelancers raise rates by a modest percentage once a year. It's predictable, easy to explain and rarely loses anyone.
  • A bigger jump for new clients only. New clients have no "old price" to compare against, so this is where you can move straight to your new rate.
  • Round to a clean number. $85 sounds deliberate. $83.50 sounds like a calculation.

If the gap between your current rate and your minimum is large, don't try to close it in one step with existing clients. Move new clients to the new rate now, and bring existing clients up in one or two steps.

Step 3: Start with new clients

New clients are the easiest place to raise your rates, because they only ever see one number.

  • Update every place your price appears: your website, rate card, proposal template and any platform profiles.
  • Quote the new rate with confidence. Don't apologize or explain. It's simply your rate.
  • Consider project pricing. Instead of an hourly rate, estimate the hours, add a buffer for revisions and surprises (15% is a sensible start), and quote a fixed price for the project. The client compares prices for outcomes, not your hourly figure.
  • Offer three options. A proposal with a basic, standard and premium package lets clients choose the level of spend, and many pick the middle one. The guide on how to write a freelance proposal shows how to set this up.

Step 4: Give existing clients notice

With clients you already work with, the way you raise rates matters as much as the number.

  • Give notice. 30 days is a common minimum, and 60 days is kinder for long-term or retainer clients. Check your contract in case it specifies a notice period.
  • Don't change prices mid-project. Finish the current project at the agreed fee and apply the new rate from the next one.
  • Put it in writing, with the new rate and the date it starts.
  • Give a short reason, not an apology. Increased experience, more demand, rising costs or a broader service are all normal reasons. One sentence is enough.
  • Consider grandfathering your best clients. Keeping a favorite long-term client at a slightly lower rate for a few extra months is a good-will gesture, not a weakness. Just set an end date.
  • Update the contract and invoices. Make sure the new rate appears in your next agreement and on your invoice template.

Rate increase email templates

Keep it short and friendly. These are starting points, so adjust the wording to fit how you usually write to each client.

For an existing client:

Subject: Rate update from [Month Day]

Hi Sam, thanks again for another great year of working together. I'm writing to let you know that from [Month Day], my rate for new projects will be $[new rate] per hour (currently $[old rate]). This reflects [the extra experience and services I now bring / rising business costs]. Anything we've already agreed, including the current project, stays at the current rate. I'd love to keep working together, so let me know if you have any questions.

For a retainer client:

Subject: Retainer update for [Month]

Hi Sam, a heads-up that from [Month Day], my monthly retainer will be $[new amount] for the same scope (currently $[old amount]). That gives you [60] days' notice. If it helps to adjust the scope to keep the budget the same, I'm happy to suggest options.

When a client pushes back:

Thanks for letting me know. I understand budgets are tight. I can't go below $[new rate] for this scope, but I can offer a smaller version: [reduced deliverables] for $[lower total]. Would that work better?

Notice that the pushback reply changes the scope, not the rate. If you discount your rate the first time someone asks, the increase was never real.

What if a client says no?

Some clients will leave, and that's usually fine. If one client is a large share of your income, raise rates with new clients first and build a buffer before changing theirs. The clients most sensitive to price are often the ones who also negotiate every invoice, pay late and ask for extras. Replacing one of them with a client at your new rate means more income for fewer hours.

The short version

  1. Work out your real minimum rate from your income goal, taxes, expenses and billable hours.
  2. Choose a new rate above that floor, and round it.
  3. Move new clients to the new rate straight away.
  4. Give existing clients 30–60 days' written notice, and never change the price mid-project.
  5. Send a short, friendly email with the new rate, the start date and a one-line reason.
  6. When a client pushes back, reduce the scope, not the rate.
